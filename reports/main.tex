\begin{abstract}
We build and evaluate four related generative systems and deploy them in one browser-based application. On the Oxford-IIIT Pet dataset we study image restoration under three runtime corruptions (salt-and-pepper noise, Gaussian blur, rectangular occlusion): (i) a universal convolutional autoencoder with a genuine bottleneck, (ii) a hard-routed system in which a corruption classifier selects one of three specialist autoencoders, and (iii) a jointly fine-tuned soft mixture-of-experts (MoE) with a differentiable gate and an identity branch. On the FS2K dataset we train (iv) a style-conditioned U-Net/PatchGAN face-to-sketch generator. On a fixed test manifest of 36{,}690 corrupted images the classifier reaches 99.8\% accuracy, hard routing reaches 26.56\,dB PSNR / 0.804 SSIM and soft MoE reaches 26.91\,dB / 0.828 against 22.34\,dB / 0.671 for the unrestored inputs (the overall averages include clean images). The face-to-sketch generator reaches 0.486 SSIM on the official test set and produces visibly blurry sketches, which we analyse. All hyperparameters were tuned with Optuna, experiments were tracked with Weights \& Biases, all inference models were exported to ONNX and verified against PyTorch (maximum absolute difference $1.3\times10^{-6}$), and everything is served through a FastAPI backend and a React frontend started with a single Docker Compose command.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Real-world images are rarely degraded in one known way. A restoration system must therefore either handle every corruption with one model, decide which specialist to apply, or blend specialists. This work compares these three designs under identical data, splits and metrics, and adds a paired image-to-image generation task. We also treat the project as a software product: every model is exported to ONNX and reachable through one web application.

Our contributions are: (1) a reproducible runtime-corruption pipeline with deterministic validation and test manifests; (2) a documented design history of the universal autoencoder (v1--v3) with controlled ablations; (3) a hard-routed system evaluated with both oracle and predicted routing; (4) a soft MoE initialised from the hard-routed components, with an analysis of its gate; (5) a style-conditioned cGAN with a style embedding used in both generator and discriminator; and (6) a containerised application whose models match their PyTorch originals.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Restoration with autoencoders.} Denoising autoencoders learn to reconstruct clean inputs from corrupted ones \cite{vincent2008}; convolutional denoisers such as DnCNN \cite{zhang2017dncnn} and inpainting networks \cite{pathak2016} extend this to images. Skip connections as in U-Net \cite{ronneberger2015} preserve detail but, when unrestricted, let a network copy its input, which defeats a compression bottleneck. Combining an $L_1$ term with a structural-similarity term is a well-studied loss for restoration \cite{zhao2017loss,wang2004ssim}.

\textbf{Mixtures of experts.} Mixtures of experts combine specialists through a gating network \cite{jacobs1991}. Sparse gating at scale \cite{shazeer2017} and later work \cite{fedus2022} motivate auxiliary losses that keep expert usage balanced; we use a differentiable balance term for the same reason.

\textbf{Conditional generation.} GANs \cite{goodfellow2014} and their conditional form \cite{mirza2014} underlie paired translation with a U-Net generator and PatchGAN discriminator \cite{isola2017}. Feature-wise linear modulation (FiLM) \cite{perez2018} injects a condition into intermediate features, which we use for the style embedding. FS2K \cite{fan2022fs2k} provides photo--sketch pairs in three styles.

\textbf{Tooling.} Hyperparameters were optimised with Optuna \cite{akiba2019}, experiments tracked with Weights \& Biases \cite{biewald2020}, and models served with ONNX Runtime \cite{onnxrt}.

\section{Datasets and Experimental Setup}
\subsection{Oxford-IIIT Pet (Tasks 1--3)}
We use the Oxford-IIIT Pet dataset \cite{parkhi2012}. Images are converted to RGB and resized to $128\times128$. The official \texttt{trainval} collection (3{,}680 images) is split 80/20 with random seed 42 into 2{,}944 training and 736 validation images (no overlap). The official test set (3{,}669 images) was held out. Class labels are unused: the clean image is the target. The same split is used for Tasks 1--3.

\subsection{Runtime corruptions}
During training the data loader draws, with equal probability, one of four input conditions for every loaded image and samples fresh parameters from operating-system entropy, so no corrupted copies are stored. Table~\ref{tab:corr} gives the definitions. The condition label is returned with each sample and trains the classifier of Task 2. Training batches for the classifier and the soft MoE are exactly balanced (verified: \texttt{[8, 8, 8, 8]} per batch of 32).

\subsection{Deterministic manifests}
Validation and test corruptions are generated once and stored in JSON manifests (type, severity, blur settings, rectangle coordinates and seed per entry). The validation manifest has 736 entries (exactly 184 per condition). The test manifest has $3{,}669\times10=36{,}690$ entries: each clean test image appears once clean and under each corruption at its three fixed severities. Occlusion rectangles are sampled without overlap so the stated area is respected.

\subsection{FS2K (Task 4)}
FS2K \cite{fan2022fs2k} contains 2{,}104 photo--sketch pairs; we use the official split of 1{,}058 training and 1{,}046 test pairs. Fifteen percent of the training portion (159 pairs) forms a validation set, stratified by style with seed 42, leaving 899 training pairs (validation: 54/52/53 per style; official test: 619/381/46). Photographs and sketches are resized to $128\times128$ and pairing is preserved; sketches are grayscale, so the generator outputs one channel. The style label is the \texttt{style} field of the annotation file. A cross-tabulation showed that the three photo folders are source subsets, not styles (folder \texttt{photo1} mixes styles 0 and 1), so the folder name was not used as the condition. A correlation check supports correct pairing (matched pairs 0.445 versus 0.243 for shifted and 0.207 for shuffled pairs). Spatial augmentation is limited to a horizontal flip applied identically to both images.

\subsection{Metrics, optimisation and tracking}
We report PSNR $=10\log_{10}(1/\mathrm{MSE})$ with MSE clamped at $10^{-5}$, so PSNR is capped at 50\,dB (this cap makes clean images count as 50\,dB when no restoration is applied), and SSIM (Gaussian window 11, data range 1). Every table also lists the metrics of the unrestored input as a baseline. ``Overall'' rows average all conditions, including clean inputs, and should be read with this cap in mind; the per-condition rows are the meaningful comparison.

All models use AdamW (Task 4: Adam with $\beta_1=0.5$), cosine learning-rate schedules and seed 42. Hyperparameters were tuned with Optuna \cite{akiba2019} (TPE sampler, seed 42, median pruner, SQLite storage); the objective for restoration tasks is $J=0.5\,\ssim+0.5\,\mathrm{PSNR}/40$ on the validation manifest. Metrics, losses, checkpoints and sample images were logged to Weights \& Biases \cite{biewald2020}. Training ran on a Tesla T4 GPU in Google Colab.

\textbf{Test-set protocol.} All hyperparameter and checkpoint selection used the validation split only. The official test set was nevertheless evaluated for earlier model versions (v1, v2) for comparison, and the design of v3 was made after seeing those results. No parameter was tuned on test data.

\section{Task 1: Universal Denoising Autoencoder}
\subsection{Architecture}
A single autoencoder restores clean, noisy, blurred and occluded inputs and is never told the corruption type. The encoder has four stride-2 blocks (each: $4{\times}4$ convolution, batch normalisation, LeakyReLU, then a $3{\times}3$ convolution with the same activation) taking $128{\times}128{\times}3$ to $8{\times}8{\times}256$ with channels $32,64,128,256$. A $1{\times}1$ convolution compresses this to a latent of $64{\times}8{\times}8=4096$ values, a $12\times$ reduction of the 49{,}152 input values, followed by dropout. The decoder mirrors the encoder with four transposed-convolution blocks and ends in a sigmoid. The final model has \emph{no} skip connections (Fig.~\ref{fig:ae}).

\subsection{Loss and training}
With clean image $x$ and reconstruction $\hat{x}$ the loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\alpha\,\lone(x,\hat{x})+(1-\alpha)\bigl(1-\ssim(x,\hat{x})\bigr).
\end{equation}
The $L_1$ term drives accurate pixel values and the SSIM term preserves structure. The final model was trained for 100 epochs with batch size 32, learning rate $4.55\times10^{-4}$, dropout 0.1 and $\alpha=0.6$ (selected by Optuna).

\subsection{Hyperparameter search and design history}
The design evolved over three Optuna studies, each motivated by the previous result (Table~\ref{tab:t1hist}). \textbf{v1} used a flattened linear bottleneck (up to 512 values). Its test PSNR was nearly flat across inputs (about 17.4--18.1\,dB, even for clean images), indicating that the vector bottleneck, not training length, limited quality: 60 epochs gave only 0.447 against 0.395 for an 8-epoch trial. \textbf{v2} replaced the vector by a spatial $8{\times}8$ latent that keeps the image layout. \textbf{v3} added a narrow skip connection to the search space (width 0, 2, 4 or 8) to test whether limited skips help.

\subsection{Are skip connections needed? A controlled ablation}
The assignment permits limited skips if investigated. In 15-epoch runs with otherwise identical settings, a narrow skip helped (Table~\ref{tab:abl}): width 4 and 8 reached 0.606 and 0.610 against 0.575 and 0.569 without a skip, and widening the latent alone (64 vs.\ 32 channels) did not help. However, short runs favour skips, which speed up early learning. We therefore trained the Optuna-best configuration for 100 epochs with and without the skip: the no-skip model scored \textbf{0.646} (SSIM 0.745, PSNR 21.88\,dB) and the skip model 0.639 (SSIM 0.734, PSNR 21.75\,dB). The gap is small and based on one seed each, so we do not claim the skip hurts, only that it gives no benefit at convergence. We chose the \textbf{no-skip model} as the final Task 1 model: it is at least as accurate, simpler, and satisfies the bottleneck requirement without needing justification. Skip width 4 adds 1{,}024 values (a $16{\times}16{\times}4$ map), well below the 4{,}096-value latent.

\textbf{Interpretation.} The universal model clearly helps for salt-and-pepper noise (SSIM 0.382$\to$0.760 and PSNR 16.4$\to$22.3\,dB, with 0.204$\to$0.745 SSIM at the highest severity). Its output quality is almost independent of the input: about 22.2--22.5\,dB for clean inputs and for all three noise severities. This is the signature of a capacity ceiling set by the bottleneck, not of condition-specific restoration. The cost is visible on inputs that need no restoration: clean images drop to 22.45\,dB and mild blur drops from 32.34 to 22.54\,dB, so for Gaussian blur the output is below the input in both PSNR and SSIM at every severity. Occlusion is the only condition where output quality depends on severity (PSNR 21.11\,dB at low severity versus 18.72\,dB at high); PSNR improves over the input (13.59$\to$19.96\,dB), but overall SSIM is lower (0.709$\to$0.678) because unaffected regions are blurred. Compared with the design history (Table~\ref{tab:t1hist}), moving from the vector to the spatial latent raised test SSIM from 0.439 to 0.639, and the final model reached 0.735. These limits motivate Tasks 2 and 3, where clean inputs bypass restoration.

\section{Task 2: Corruption Classification and Hard-Routed Specialists}
\subsection{System overview}
Instead of asking one autoencoder to solve every restoration problem, a CNN classifier predicts the corruption type and a matching specialist autoencoder restores the image (Fig.~\ref{fig:hard}). A clean input uses an identity bypass and is never processed by a restoration expert:
\begin{equation}
\hat{x}=\begin{cases} x_c, & k=\text{clean},\\ E_k(x_c), & k\in\{\text{salt},\text{blur},\text{occ}\},\end{cases}\qquad k=\arg\max_i p_i .
\end{equation}

\subsection{Corruption classifier}
The classifier has four blocks (two $3{\times}3$ convolutions with batch normalisation \cite{ioffe2015} and ReLU, then $2{\times}2$ max-pooling) with channels $16,32,64,128$, followed by concatenated global average and global max pooling (256 features), dropout 0.5 and a linear layer with four outputs (295{,}028 parameters). Max pooling helps detect isolated noise pixels and black rectangles. It is trained with multiclass cross-entropy on labels produced automatically by the runtime corruption pipeline. Batches are exactly balanced: the class of sample $i$ is $i \bmod 4$ and shuffling is disabled, so every batch of 32 holds eight images per class (verified by printing the first batch).

\textbf{Optuna.} The search space was learning rate $[3{\times}10^{-4},3{\times}10^{-3}]$ (log), batch size $\{16,32,64\}$, channel configuration $\{$small, medium, large$\}$, dropout $[0,0.5]$ and weight decay $[10^{-6},10^{-2}]$ (log), with validation macro-F1 as the objective. Of 30 trials, 6 completed and 24 were pruned; the best validation macro-F1 was 0.9986 with learning rate $7.1{\times}10^{-4}$, batch size 16, the \emph{small} configuration, dropout 0.5 and weight decay $2.5{\times}10^{-4}$. The final model was trained for 40 epochs (validation macro-F1 1.000, accuracy 1.000).

\subsection{Specialist autoencoders}
Each specialist uses the Task 1 backbone (about 2.5\,M parameters, 10.1\,MB as ONNX) and is trained only on its own corruption with the same clean target. A shared Optuna search identified a common architecture on a proxy task (one autoencoder trained on the union of the three corruptions), after which the three specialists were trained independently with their own data, seed (42 plus the class index) and parameters. The search space was learning rate $[5{\times}10^{-5},2{\times}10^{-3}]$, batch size $\{16,32,64\}$, base channels $\{32,48,64\}$, latent channels $\{16,32,48,64\}$, skip width $\{0,2,4,8\}$ and $\alpha\in[0.5,0.95]$. Of 12 trials, 6 completed and 6 were pruned; the best (objective 0.5999) used learning rate $9.0{\times}10^{-4}$, batch size 16, base 32, latent 48 channels ($8{\times}8$, 3072 values), skip width 8 (a $16{\times}16{\times}8$ map, 2048 values, below the latent size) and $\alpha=0.6$. The four best completed trials all used skip width 8 and the two completed trials without a skip scored lowest. Because short trials favour skips (Section~IV-C), this evidence is weaker than the Task 1 control, and we did not run a no-skip control for the specialists. Each specialist was trained for 60 epochs.

\section{Task 3: Jointly Trained Soft Mixture-of-Experts}
\subsection{Architecture}
A hard decision is brittle near class boundaries and cannot express mild corruption. The soft MoE keeps the four branches of Task 2 (identity, three specialists) but replaces the arg-max with a continuous weighting by a gating network $g$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:moe}):
\begin{equation}
w=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{g(x_c)}{T}\right),\qquad
\hat{x}=w_c\,x_c+w_s E_{\mathrm{salt}}(x_c)+w_b E_{\mathrm{blur}}(x_c)+w_o E_{\mathrm{occ}}(x_c).
\end{equation}
The mixture is differentiable, so gate and experts are trained jointly through the final reconstruction error. The gate is initialised from the Task 2 classifier, whose four outputs are already ordered as (clean, salt, blur, occlusion), and the experts from the three trained specialists; nothing is initialised randomly.

\subsection{Loss and training schedule}
The joint loss combines reconstruction, structure, classification and a routing-balance term:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\lambda_1\lone+\lambda_2(1-\ssim)+\lambda_3\,\mathcal{L}_{CE}+\lambda_4\sum_{i=1}^{4}\bigl(\bar{w}_i-\tfrac14\bigr)^2 ,
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_1=\alpha$, $\lambda_2=1-\alpha$, $\lambda_3=\lambda_{cls}$ and $\lambda_4=\lambda_{bal}$. The cross-entropy is computed on the tempered logits $g/T$, so it constrains the same weights the mixture uses, and $\bar{w}_i$ is the mean weight of branch $i$ in an exactly balanced batch (target $1/4$ each, as in switch-style load balancing \cite{shazeer2017,fedus2022}). Training has two phases. In a 3-epoch \textbf{warm-up} the experts are frozen and kept in evaluation mode (fixed batch-normalisation statistics) and only the gate is trained at learning rate $5{\times}10^{-4}$. In 20 \textbf{joint} epochs all parameters are fine-tuned at the smaller learning rate $\eta$ with cosine decay. A trial is pruned if any branch's mean validation weight falls below 0.02 (routing collapse); none occurred.

\subsection{Optuna search}
The search covered $\eta\in[10^{-5},2{\times}10^{-4}]$ (log), $T\in[0.5,3]$, $\lambda_{cls}\in[0.01,1]$ (log), $\lambda_{bal}\in[10^{-3},0.5]$ (log) and $\alpha\in[0.5,0.95]$, with the validation objective of Section~III-E and 1 warm-up plus 4 joint epochs per trial. All 10 trials completed (none pruned) and their objectives lay between 0.7998 and 0.8114, so the model is insensitive to these hyperparameters. The best trial used $\eta=1.13{\times}10^{-4}$, $T=1.26$, $\lambda_{cls}=0.016$, $\lambda_{bal}=0.070$ and $\alpha=0.7$. The final model reached its best validation score (0.8164) at joint epoch 16 of 20.

\section{Task 4: Style-Conditioned Face-to-Sketch Generation}
\subsection{Architecture}
The generator is a U-Net that maps a photograph $x$ and a style $c\in\{1,2,3\}$ to a one-channel sketch $\hat{s}=G(x,c)$. A learned embedding of the style (32 dimensions) is used in two ways: it is broadcast over the image and concatenated to the input as extra channels, and it drives feature-wise linear modulation \cite{perez2018} in every decoder block (a per-channel scale and shift predicted from the embedding, initialised to the identity). The encoder has six stride-2 blocks (base 48 channels, widths $b,2b,4b,8b,8b,8b$ down to a $2{\times}2$ map) and the decoder five transposed-convolution blocks with skip connections, dropout in the first three, and a final transposed convolution with $\tanh$ (about 16.5\,M parameters). The PatchGAN discriminator receives the photograph, the sketch and its own style embedding (broadcast as channels) and outputs a $14{\times}14$ map of patch logits, so the style condition is part of both networks and not only an interface label.

\subsection{Objective and training}
With binary cross-entropy on logits, the discriminator minimises $\tfrac12(\mathcal{L}_{real}+\mathcal{L}_{fake})$ where real pairs $(x,s,c)$ are labelled 1 and generated pairs $(x,G(x,c),c)$ are labelled 0, and the generator minimises
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_G=\mathcal{L}_{adv}+\lambda_{L1}\,\lone\bigl(s,G(x,c)\bigr).
\end{equation}
We use Adam \cite{kingma2015} with $\beta=(0.5,0.999)$ and separate learning rates for $G$ and $D$. The discriminator real and fake losses, the generator adversarial and $L_1$ losses and the validation metrics (L1, PSNR, SSIM and a style-sensitivity measure) are logged separately each epoch, and a fixed set of validation photographs is rendered with the true style and all three styles at regular intervals (to Weights \& Biases) so progress can be compared over time.

\subsection{Hyperparameter search}
The space was generator learning rate $[10^{-4},10^{-3}]$, discriminator learning rate $[5{\times}10^{-5},5{\times}10^{-4}]$, batch size $\{8,16,32\}$, base channels $\{32,48,64\}$, dropout $[0,0.5]$, style-embedding dimension $\{8,16,32\}$ and $\lambda_{L1}\in\{10,25,50,100,200\}$, with validation SSIM as the objective and 15 epochs per trial. Of 12 trials, 4 completed and 8 were pruned. The best (objective 0.5006) had $\eta_G=5.96{\times}10^{-4}$, $\eta_D=4.35{\times}10^{-4}$, batch size 32, base 48, dropout 0.1, embedding dimension 32 and $\lambda_{L1}=200$; it was retrained for the full 100 epochs (best validation SSIM 0.508, PSNR 16.14\,dB, L1 0.095).

\textbf{Does the style condition matter?} Generating the same photograph under different styles changes the output by a mean absolute difference of 0.102 (on a $[0,1]$ scale). \subsection{Limitations and an ablation on the reconstruction weight}
The generated sketches are soft and blurry: they capture the head shape, hair mass, eyes and mouth but not the line character (individual strokes, hatching) of the real sketches, and a faint grid texture is visible in dark areas, consistent with transposed-convolution upsampling. The elderly subject in the third style comparison is rendered much more faintly than the others, and Style 2 turns his features into a messy blur. We tested whether the large reconstruction weight, which encourages averaging, was the cause by retraining the best configuration with $\lambda_{L1}=50$ (Table~\ref{tab:t4abl}). Test metrics were unchanged within run-to-run noise (SSIM 0.486 versus 0.484), and the sketches looked equally soft, so the hypothesis is \emph{not supported}. We kept $\lambda_{L1}=200$, which was selected by the search. The training logs suggest other causes. The discriminator losses settle near 0.5 (real 0.52, fake 0.51 at epoch 100), so the adversarial signal is modest. Training L1 falls from 0.298 to 0.113 while validation L1 stays near 0.100 from epoch 10 on, and validation SSIM peaks around epoch 20 (0.508) before drifting down to 0.469, so the model stops improving on unseen faces early; we therefore keep the best-validation checkpoint. (Training L1 is measured with dropout active, so it is not directly comparable to validation L1.) The hand-drawn sketches are also only loosely aligned with the photographs, which penalises sharp lines under pixel-wise losses. These are hypotheses supported by the logs; we did not isolate them with further experiments. The four lowest-SSIM test pairs (indices 221, 65 and 42 in Style 2 and 517 in Style 1; SSIM 0.17--0.21) all have real sketches with dense individual strokes (hair strands, shading on the cheeks and neck), whereas the generated sketch is a soft blur that keeps the dark hair mass, eyes and mouth but no strokes; in the Style 1 case the output is very faint, almost a blank face.

\subsection{Architecture}
The frontend (React 18, Vite and Tailwind CSS) is built in a Node container and served by nginx, which also proxies \texttt{/api/} to a FastAPI backend (Python~3.12) that runs the seven ONNX models with ONNX Runtime on CPU. The backend validates every upload (type, a 10\,MB size limit and decodability; errors return 400, 413, 415, 422 or 503 with a readable message), resizes images to $128{\times}128$, applies the requested corruption with the same functions and fixed severities as the evaluation (seeded and reproducible), runs inference and returns the input, restored image, optional clean original, the corruption settings, PSNR when a clean reference exists, routing probabilities or weights, and timings. The endpoints are \texttt{GET /api/health}, \texttt{GET /api/samples}, \texttt{POST /api/universal}, \texttt{POST /api/hard} (predicted or oracle routing), \texttt{POST /api/soft} and \texttt{POST /api/sketch}. Thirteen automated backend tests pass, including a check that the seeded corruption settings match the specification table. Model files are not stored in Git; a script downloads them from a shared folder.

\subsection{Workspaces and deployment}
The four workspaces (Fig.~\ref{fig:app}) accept an uploaded image, a bundled sample or (for sketches) a webcam capture. In \emph{Universal Restoration} the user uploads an already corrupted image or applies a corruption and severity to a clean sample, and sees the input, the restored output, the settings and the inference time. \emph{Hard-Routed Restoration} shows the four classifier probabilities, the predicted corruption, the selected expert (or ``identity bypass''), an oracle/predicted switch and the timing split. \emph{Soft MoE} shows the four routing weights, marks the dominant expert and lists the contributing branches, with the temperature displayed as fixed. \emph{Face-to-Sketch} shows the photograph and sketch side by side for Style 1, 2 or 3 and offers a PNG download; every restoration result can also be downloaded. The whole system starts with one command, \texttt{docker compose up --build}, after \texttt{python scripts/download\_models.py}, and is opened at \texttt{http://localhost:8080}; a container health check on the backend delays the frontend until the models are loaded.

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Capacity versus fidelity.} A genuine compression bottleneck cannot reproduce fine detail: even clean images are restored at only about 22\,dB by the universal model, so restoration can be worse than the input for mild corruption (blur) and for clean images. The identity bypass (Task 2) and the identity branch (Task 3) are the remedy, and even the soft MoE stays below the input for the mildest blur.
\item \textbf{Single seeds and short searches.} Every number comes from one run with seed 42. Differences of a few thousandths (for example skip versus no-skip, 0.639 versus 0.646, or $\lambda_{L1}=50$ versus 200) are within plausible seed noise. The Optuna studies are small (8--30 trials, short trials, a median pruner), several optima lay on the edge of the searched range, and the Task 3 search was nearly flat.
\item \textbf{Proxy search and missing controls.} The specialist architecture was found with one autoencoder trained on all three corruptions, not per corruption, and there is no no-skip control at convergence for the specialists.
\item \textbf{Test-set use.} Earlier model versions (v1, v2) were also evaluated on the test set before v3 was designed; all selection used validation data only.
\item \textbf{Metrics.} PSNR is capped at 50\,dB, which inflates the overall averages whenever clean inputs are left unchanged; per-condition rows are the fair comparison.
\item \textbf{Face-to-sketch quality.} The sketches are blurry; validation SSIM peaks early and declines, and the hand-drawn targets are only loosely aligned with the photographs; the official test split is imbalanced (619/381/46), so Style 3 is barely evaluated.
\item \textbf{Application.} Inference runs on CPU; the soft MoE takes about 75\,ms per image in the benchmark (single images in the container can be slower on a first request). The webcam requires a secure context (localhost or HTTPS), and the model files must be downloaded separately from the repository.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
We compared three restoration designs and a style-conditioned cGAN under identical data splits and metrics, and delivered them as one containerised application. A real compression bottleneck limits a single universal autoencoder to SSIM 0.735; routing each image to a specialist raised this to 0.804 with a 99.8\%-accurate classifier, and a jointly fine-tuned soft mixture-of-experts reached 26.91\,dB and 0.828, with the gains concentrated at low corruption severity where the gate learned to mix in the identity branch. All experts remained active and specific, and the gate traded some classification accuracy (88.4\%) for better restoration. The design evolved through controlled experiments (vector versus spatial bottleneck, narrow skip versus no skip, reconstruction weight), several of which overturned the initial expectation: a skip connection helped in short runs but not at convergence, and lowering the L1 weight did not sharpen the generated sketches. The face-to-sketch generator responds clearly to the style condition but produces blurry sketches (test SSIM 0.486), the weakest result. All models match their PyTorch versions after ONNX export (maximum difference $1.3\times10^{-6}$) and run behind a FastAPI/React application started with Docker Compose. Future work includes a perceptual or feature-matching loss and data augmentation for the cGAN, per-severity experts, and multi-seed evaluation of the architectural comparisons.
